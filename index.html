A Realm Divided

For centuries, the lands of Tiānxià (All Under Heaven) stood beneath a single throne. Thirty-one years ago, a disaster claimed the imperial household. Powerful provincial rulers, commanders, and noble families contested the succession, and the great empire fractured into rival states.

Today five powerful nations seek influence, security, and ultimately the authority to reunite the realm. Their rulers observe a temporary truce to mourn the philosopher Yú Tiānmíng (Yu Tienming), even as soldiers remain on the borders and rumors of renewed war fill the roads.

Most people spend their lives farming, trading, fishing, studying, crafting, or serving in local government. Martial masters who consciously channel Qì (Qi) are rare enough to attract admiration, fear, and rumor.

What ordinary citizens generally know

There are five rival major powers; the imperial household died in the Night of Falling Stars; merchants and refugees still suffer from war; renowned martial sects teach extraordinary skills; the natural and spiritual worlds are linked through Qì (Qi).

Education and geography matter. A city scholar may know more about courts and philosophy, while a rural villager knows more about local customs and the nearby sects.

Public History of Tiānxià

These are the events taught by scholars, repeated by traveling storytellers, and remembered in family histories.

The Imperial Centuries

Tiānmìng Dìguó (Celestial Empire) unified the continent under Lóng Shì (House Long). Provincial governors, examination scholars, magistrates, generals, and noble families administered the vast state. Powerful martial schools flourished alongside its cities and temples.

Thirty-One Years Ago — Zhuìxīng Zhī Yè (Night of Falling Stars)

During a winter-solstice ceremony, a catastrophic earthquake destroyed the main imperial audience hall. Emperor Lóng Zhèngyuán (Long Zhengyuan), the empress, and their four princes died. Official accounts classify the tragedy as a natural disaster.

The Succession Wars

Without an accepted heir, provincial leaders disputed the mandate to govern. Alliances broke, territories declared independence, and decades of warfare reshaped the former provinces into competing nations.

The Rise of Five Courts

Five major regional governments consolidated power: the Jade Regency, Crimson Shogunate, Azure Mountain Confederacy, Verdant Court, and Golden Khanate. Each inherited part of the old realm, but none has reunified it.

Recent Days — The Death of the Sage

The beloved philosopher Yú Tiānmíng (Yu Tienming), known as the Sage of the Common Road, died at the age of ninety-three. His final request for forty-nine days without offensive war was accepted by the rival courts.

A tale from antiquity

Some storytellers recount the legend of Wáng Wěi (Wang Wei), an ancient martial hero said to have challenged Heaven itself. Versions differ greatly: to some he is a cautionary figure of pride, to others a symbol of mortal defiance. His story belongs to folktale and philosophical debate, not to present-day political news.

Why the courts disagree

The Jade Regency stresses the authority of the old capital and civil administration. The Crimson Shogunate emphasizes military duty and stability. The Azure Confederacy questions whether heredity alone grants legitimacy. The Verdant Court insists on protecting sacred lands and local agreements. The Golden Khanate demands full recognition for frontier peoples.

Each position has supporters and critics. Neither a noble family's claim nor a martial master's fame settles the matter for everyone.

Life Beneath the Five Banners

Villages and towns

Farming families, artisans, fishers, shopkeepers, and laborers anchor local life. Taxes, security, harvests, and kinship matter more to most than distant succession claims.

Officials and scholars

Magistrates, scribes, exam-trained officials, and advisers handle law, trade, records, and public works. Their authority varies by nation.

Warriors and retainers

Soldiers, guards, professional escorts, samurai retainers, and wandering fighters may serve rulers, clans, employers, or personal codes of duty.

Merchants and travelers

Caravans and river traders link the regions. Border inspections, rising prices, and displaced people are everyday consequences of the wars.

Temples and spirits

Many communities maintain ancestral shrines and honor local guardians. Stories of river, mountain, and woodland spirits are widespread, though encounters differ greatly.

Martial schools

Independent sects train fighters in weapons, discipline, meditation, and sometimes Qì (Qi). Some claim neutrality; others maintain longstanding ties to courts.

Common points of disagreement

People argue about whether the old empire should be restored, which taxes are justified, whether martial sects should answer to monarchs, and how much authority spirits should have over human settlements. These are ordinary public debates rather than hidden mysteries.

Qì (Qi) and the Jiānghú (Jianghu, Martial World)

Qì (Qi) is understood as the living current present in people and nature. Most people do not consciously manipulate it. Disciplined practitioners, called xiūliàn zhě (cultivators), spend years pursuing physical, mental, and spiritual refinement.

The highest masters are subjects of legends. Common people may know the names of the cultivation realms without witnessing most of their purported powers.

The Nine Cultivation Realms

1 Fán Tǐ (Mortal Body) — Conventional martial training and physical discipline.

2 Qì Juéxǐng (Qi Awakening) — Initial perception and conscious circulation of Qi.

3 Jīngmài Kāiqǐ (Meridian Opening) — Refinement of internal circulation and technique.

4 Zhù Jī (Foundation Establishment) — A stable basis for more remarkable martial arts.

5 Jīn Dān (Golden Core) — A powerful internal reserve of refined Qi.

6 Yuán Yīng (Nascent Soul) — Highly advanced spirit and Qi practice.

7 Huà Shén (Spirit Transformation) — Legendary cultivation of body, mind, and spirit.

8 Tiān Shēng (Heavenly Ascension) — Near-mythic mastery associated with celestial stories.

9 Chāofán Xiān (Transcendent Immortal) — A largely legendary state beyond ordinary mortal limits.

Martial philosophies

Shān Zhī Dào (Path of the Mountain)

Physical strength, direct force, and decisive impact.

Fēng Zhī Dào (Path of the Wind)

Precision, balance, speed, and agile movement.

Tiě Gēn Zhī Dào (Path of the Iron Root)

Endurance, conditioning, and bodily resilience.

Qiān Xíng Zhī Dào (Path of the Thousand Forms)

Study, calculation, and the structure of Qì.

Liú Dào Zhī Lù (Path of the Flowing Dao)

Harmony, perception, healing, and redirection.

Jūn Hún Zhī Lù (Path of the Sovereign Soul)

Conviction, presence, and the force of the spirit.

Five phases of elemental cultivation

Mù (Wood) — growth and renewal; Huǒ (Fire) — transformation and intensity; Tǔ (Earth) — stability and endurance; Jīn (Metal) — structure and precision; Shuǐ (Water) — adaptability and flow.

Different schools interpret these principles differently. A school of swordsmanship and a school of healing may both cultivate the same elemental phase.

The Forty-Nine-Day Mourning Truce

The Wǔshí Zhī Yuē (Accord of Forty-Nine Days) suspends offensive military action while the courts mourn Yú Tiānmíng (Yu Tienming). It does not erase past disputes or settle who should inherit the Dragon Throne.

“Grant the realm forty-nine days in which no mother must bury her child for the ambition of a throne.” — traditionally attributed to Yú Tiānmíng

What everyone sees

At court

Delegations attend memorial rites, exchange formal messages, and negotiate trade and border concerns.

On the roads

Caravans carry supplies, soldiers inspect travelers, and families displaced by earlier fighting seek shelter.

In the villages

People honor the philosopher, debate the coming season, and worry about grain levies, conscription, and renewed fighting.

Among martial sects

Schools accept pupils and attend public festivals, while independent warriors consider whom they will serve if war returns.

The Grand Assembly of Mourning

At the close of the truce, representatives of the major courts are expected at Qiānzhōng Sì (Monastery of a Thousand Bells) for the last memorial ceremonies. The gathering is regarded as an opportunity for diplomacy before the accord expires.
